# EatFood: Business Growth Plan

Planning date: 19 October 2026  
Market: Local food businesses in India  
Status: A plan to test, not proof of demand or a revenue forecast

## 1. Business direction

Build a web app that turns a restaurant's direct WhatsApp orders into clear kitchen tickets, payment records, customer updates, and bills.

Start by helping businesses handle orders they already receive. Then test whether easier reordering, local discovery, and customer referrals produce additional profitable orders.

Initial promise:

> Your customers order through your WhatsApp. Your staff receive a clear order ticket, track payment, update the customer, and generate the bill in one place.

Longer-term promise, only after measuring results:

> Manage direct orders and give existing customers an easier way to order again.

Do not promise guaranteed sales, automatic migration away from food marketplaces, or a particular amount of commission savings.

## 2. Separate the two growth problems

| Growth problem | Who needs to act | What success means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Acquire restaurants that pay for EatFood | Founder and later sales partners | Restaurants pay, use the product, and renew |
| Help those restaurants acquire and retain diners | Restaurant, supported by EatFood | Profitable completed orders and repeat purchases |

An order link does not generate traffic on its own. Restaurants need an audience, distribution, or an acquisition budget. EatFood must make those efforts easier to execute and measure.

## 3. First customer segment

Choose one locality and one segment for the first pilot. A reasonable starting hypothesis is a local meal or takeaway business with repeat customers and existing WhatsApp orders.

Good pilot characteristics:

- Receives direct orders regularly and can show recent examples.
- Staff currently copy messages into paper tickets or another system.
- Experiences missed orders, wrong items, payment confusion, or repeated status questions.
- Has its own delivery arrangement or can start with pickup.
- Has an owner willing to test during real operating hours.
- Can receive payments into its own merchant account.

Possible segments to compare later: tiffin and meal businesses, takeaway restaurants, bakeries with scheduled orders, and small cafes with a local repeat audience. Each has different requirements; do not support all of them at once.

Lower-priority prospects initially:

- Restaurants expecting EatFood to bring an entirely new customer base immediately.
- Businesses with very few direct orders and little operational pain.
- Restaurants requiring full POS replacement or aggregator integration before trying anything.
- Multi-outlet chains requiring complex permissions, reporting, and procurement.

## 4. Validate demand through a concrete demonstration

### Prospecting and demo

1. Build a list of 15–20 suitable businesses in the chosen locality.
2. Visit outside their busiest service hours.
3. Ask to see the current ordering process and one recent problem.
4. With permission, prepare a demo using their menu and sample customer data.
5. Show an order placed on a phone arriving on the restaurant dashboard.
6. Change its status, show the customer update, and generate a sample bill.
7. Offer a clearly scoped paid pilot with stated setup, support, price, and exit terms.

Questions worth asking:

- How many direct orders did you receive yesterday and last week?
- Who reads the messages, confirms payment, and informs the kitchen?
- What happens when an item is unavailable or the owner is away?
- Who delivers, what does it cost, and who handles complaints?
- What software do you already use, and would this create duplicate entry?
- What result would justify paying to continue next month?

Suggested validation target: three unrelated paying pilot businesses. This is an internal decision rule, not a market benchmark. Record refusals and reasons as carefully as successful demos.

### Demo script

> You already get orders on WhatsApp. Let me show you how one becomes a clear kitchen ticket, with payment status and customer updates, without staff copying the message by hand.

Avoid selling a long future feature list. Sell the current working workflow.

## 5. Deliver the first pilot successfully

For each restaurant:

- Capture a baseline for direct order volume, errors, and time spent handling orders.
- Configure its menu, prices, hours, delivery area, charges, and payment options.
- Test acceptance, rejection, offline handling, printing, and a real payment where enabled.
- Train the staff who will actually handle orders, not only the owner.
- Agree on support hours and a fallback process before launch.
- Review results weekly and ask for paid continuation at the agreed renewal date.

Treat the first month as a suggested pilot length, not a requirement. Avoid an indefinite free trial. Any discount or free period should have a clear end and conversion price.

Record founder time spent on setup, visits, and support. A pilot that works only with constant founder intervention has not established a repeatable business.

## 6. Help restaurants bring diners into the ordering flow

### A. Convert existing attention into direct orders

- Put a restaurant-specific QR code on the counter, receipts, and appropriate takeaway packaging.
- Add the ordering link to the restaurant's Instagram bio and relevant posts.
- Let the owner share its menu or a specific offer through WhatsApp Status.
- Add a suitable ordering website link to its Google Business Profile where supported.
- Use clear messages such as “Order your next pickup here” rather than “Try our software.”

Use separate source links for counter QR, packaging, Instagram, Google, and other channels. A source identifies where an order came from; it does not prove the order was incremental.

### B. Give diners a reason to order directly

Test one offer at a time:

- A direct-order meal combo with a clear final price.
- A low-cost add-on above a minimum order value.
- Scheduled pickup for customers ordering ahead.
- A repeat-customer benefit that the restaurant can afford.

Show minimum spend, delivery charges, eligibility, and expiry before checkout. Record the cost of every offer. More discounted orders can still produce less profit.

### C. Make repeat ordering convenient

- Offer “Order again” using a previous completed order.
- Recheck prices, availability, delivery charges, and address before confirmation.
- Make recent orders easy to find without exposing one customer's details to another.
- Test scheduled meal ordering only if the chosen segment needs it.

### D. Run permission-based reactivation

- Collect permission for promotional messages separately from necessary order updates.
- Send relevant offers to appropriate customers instead of broadcasting everything to everyone.
- Start with a low frequency, honor opt-outs, and measure complaints and unsubscribes.
- Include message and provider costs when calculating campaign results.
- Use supported WhatsApp templates and messaging rules, verified before launch.

Do not treat placing an order as automatic permission for future marketing.

### E. Test referrals

- Give existing diners a shareable restaurant link or referral code.
- Apply a reward only after a qualifying completed paid order.
- Set limits and review suspicious self-referrals or repeated cancellations.
- Compare the reward cost with contribution from the referred customers.

### F. Expand through local distribution

After the ordering flow is reliable, test restaurant-led partnerships with nearby offices, hostels, shops, or residential communities, with the relevant group's permission. Use a specific lunch, pickup, or scheduled-order offer and measure results per location.

Paid advertising comes later. Start with one restaurant, one local area, a capped budget, and completed-order tracking. Include discounts and delivery subsidies in the acquisition cost. Stop campaigns that produce unprofitable orders or overwhelm the kitchen.

## 7. Turn successful restaurants into EatFood distribution

1. Obtain permission to publish a small case study with baseline, period, results, and limitations.
2. Ask satisfied owners for introductions to nearby owners.
3. Demonstrate with a working local example rather than a promise of future features.
4. Standardize menu onboarding, staff training, and payment setup.
5. Consider local POS resellers, printer vendors, or restaurant consultants as referral partners after direct sales works.

Any partner payment should follow collected restaurant revenue and have explicit terms. Do not recruit partners to sell an unproven or unreliable workflow.

## 8. Pricing and unit economics

Do not finalize pricing from the earlier conversation's WhatsApp estimates. Get current Meta/provider, payment, hosting, and support costs first. The figures below are experiments, not verified market willingness to pay.

Possible pricing tests:

| Component | Initial experiment |
| --- | --- |
| Setup | Test a separate fee if menu entry, printing, or training takes substantial time |
| Core subscription | Test a simple monthly price; ₹999 per outlet is one possible starting hypothesis |
| Promotional messages | Optional, with transparent usage charges or a clearly capped allowance |
| Delivery | Restaurant-managed or separately priced by the delivery provider |
| Future integrations | Separate pricing after confirming partner access and cost |

Do not sell unlimited messaging or unlimited personal support without understanding the cost. Display applicable taxes and any additional charges clearly.

Monthly contribution per restaurant:

> Collected subscription and usage revenue, excluding taxes − messaging/provider costs − allocated hosting − support labor − other variable costs.

Acquisition cost:

> Sales time + travel + demo setup + marketing + partner fees, divided by new paying restaurants acquired in the same cohort.

Restaurant benefit:

> Verified operating savings + contribution from genuinely additional orders + net savings on genuinely shifted orders − subscription and extra direct-order costs.

Distinguish new orders, existing phone/WhatsApp orders processed through EatFood, and marketplace orders genuinely shifted to direct ordering. Do not count every dashboard order as a new sale.

## 9. Phased roadmap and expansion gates

| Phase | Work | Evidence needed before expanding |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Demonstration | Complete the prototype in the companion specification | Owners understand the flow and agree to a defined pilot |
| 2. Paid pilots | Run with a small number of businesses in one segment | Actual completed orders, staff adoption, and willingness to pay again |
| 3. Retention | Fix missed orders, payment confusion, duplicate entry, and onboarding friction | Paid renewals and support requirements the business can afford |
| 4. Diner growth | Test source links, reordering, one offer, and referrals | Evidence of useful repeat behavior or profitable additional orders |
| 5. Repeatable local sales | Standardize setup, training, support, and owner referrals | Positive contribution and a sales process that works beyond personal favors |
| 6. Product expansion | Add the most repeated paid requirement | Enough demand to justify development and ongoing operating cost |
| 7. Geographic expansion | Repeat the same playbook in an adjacent locality | Stable service quality, retention, and viable acquisition cost |

Do not use restaurant count alone as the expansion gate. A growing number of inactive or heavily subsidized accounts can hide a weak business.

## 10. Future product options, in priority order

1. Reliable reordering and basic customer history.
2. Campaign measurement and simple referral rewards.
3. Better printer support if pilots establish a real need.
4. Scheduled orders or meal subscriptions if the initial segment needs them.
5. Integration with existing POS systems to reduce duplicate entry.
6. Kitchen display features for restaurants with sufficient order volume.
7. Multi-outlet reporting and staff permissions for customers who expand.
8. Swiggy/Zomato integration through an approved route, after checking commercial terms and access.
9. Inventory, recipe costing, loyalty automation, or delivery integrations only when recurring paid demand supports them.

This is a menu of options, not a commitment to build everything. Keep full POS replacement out of scope unless evidence justifies a separate strategy.

## 11. Weekly operating scorecard

| Area | Track |
| --- | --- |
| Restaurant sales | Qualified prospects, demos, paid pilots, reasons lost |
| Adoption | Restaurants processing actual orders, staff usage, manual fallback frequency |
| Reliability | Missed or duplicated orders, alert failures, payment mismatches, message failures |
| Retention | Eligible renewals, paid renewals, cancellations, reasons for leaving |
| Restaurant economics | Contribution per account, support minutes, setup time, acquisition cost |
| Diner behavior | Completed first orders, repeat orders, checkout abandonment |
| Growth experiments | Completed orders by source, offer cost, referral cost, estimated incremental contribution |

Define a repeat customer consistently, such as a customer with a second completed order within a stated period. Keep the same definition when comparing cohorts.

## 12. Failure signals and response

- Owners like demos but will not pay: investigate urgency, alternatives, value, and price before adding features.
- Diners open the menu but do not order: inspect charges, delivery availability, menu clarity, trust, and checkout friction.
- Staff stop using the dashboard: fix reliability or duplicate work before acquiring more restaurants.
- Orders grow but restaurant profit falls: adjust discounts, delivery terms, or acquisition channels.
- Support consumes account revenue: narrow supported setups, improve onboarding, or change pricing.
- Restaurants renew only while receiving free founder labor: revisit the business model.
